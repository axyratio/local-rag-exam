# Graduation Regulations at Ubon Ratchathani University: An Explanatory Guide

## 0. Scope, Status and How to Read This Document

This guide explains how graduation requirements are normally structured at a Thai public university, using Ubon Ratchathani University (UBU) as the frame of reference. It describes the *standard architecture* that Thai undergraduate regulations follow, derived from the national criteria issued by the Ministry of Higher Education, Science, Research and Innovation (MHESI, formerly the Office of the Higher Education Commission) together with the academic regulations each university council issues for its own institution.

A caution on status. Figures that are sector-wide national minima — the 120-credit floor for a four-year bachelor's degree, for instance — are identified as such. Figures used only to make an arithmetic example work are explicitly labelled **illustrative**. This document deliberately does not quote clause numbers or promulgation dates as though reproducing a verified legal text, because programme-specific and faculty-specific rules vary and are amended periodically. Students and staff needing an authoritative answer should consult the current UBU academic regulations and their own programme handbook (*มคอ.2* / TQF2 specification), which the Office of the Registrar publishes. What follows is intended to make the *logic* of the rules intelligible, and to let a reader who has the real numbers to hand slot them into the right places.

## 1. The Thai Undergraduate Degree Structure

### 1.1 Programme Lengths

Thai bachelor's programmes come in three normal lengths, and the length determines almost every other duration rule that follows.

| Programme type | Normal length | National minimum credits | Typical examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standard bachelor's | 4 years (8 regular semesters) | not fewer than 120 | Science, Business Administration, Liberal Arts, Nursing, most Engineering fields, Agriculture, Law, Political Science |
| Extended bachelor's | 5 years (10 regular semesters) | not fewer than 150 | Architecture, certain Fine and Applied Arts programmes, some Education programmes under older frameworks |
| Professional bachelor's | 6 years (12 regular semesters) | not fewer than 180 | Medicine, Dentistry, Veterinary Science, Pharmacy (Doctor of Pharmacy) |

Ubon Ratchathani University operates faculties across most of these categories, including Pharmaceutical Sciences (six-year), Engineering, Science, Nursing, Agriculture, Liberal Arts, Law, Political Science and Management Science. Continuing (top-up) programmes for holders of a diploma or higher vocational certificate normally run two years with not fewer than 72 credits, and carry their own credit-transfer rules.

### 1.2 The Credit System

A **credit** (*หน่วยกิต*) measures scheduled learning time across a fifteen-week semester. The national convention is:

- **Lecture / theory:** one credit = one hour per week for not fewer than fifteen weeks (15 contact hours per credit).
- **Laboratory, workshop, studio or practical work:** one credit = two to three hours per week for not fewer than fifteen weeks (30–45 contact hours per credit).
- **Field placement, clinical practice or professional practice:** one credit = three to six hours per week (45–90 contact hours per credit).
- **Independent study or project work:** one credit = three to four hours per week.

Course codes carry this in their credit notation. A module written as **3(3-0-6)** is a three-credit module with three lecture hours, no laboratory hours, and six hours of expected self-study per week. **4(3-3-7)** denotes four credits comprising three lecture hours and three laboratory hours weekly, with seven hours of private study. Students are commonly required to register for not fewer than nine and not more than twenty-two credits in a regular semester, with the dean's authorisation required to go outside that band; the summer session ceiling is normally nine credits.

### 1.3 The Academic Calendar

The Thai academic year runs from roughly June to May and comprises two regular semesters plus an optional summer session. The **first semester** typically runs from late June or early August to October or November; the **second semester** from November or January to March or April; and the **summer session** (*ภาคการศึกษาฤดูร้อน*) for not fewer than eight weeks with contact hours doubled per week so that the total learning time per credit is equivalent to a regular semester. The summer session is optional, is not counted when the regulations speak of "regular semesters," and is generally used for retaking failed modules, catching up, or accelerating.

### 1.4 Credit Composition

Every undergraduate curriculum divides its total credits into prescribed groups. The national criteria set minima for each group; programmes may exceed them.

| Credit group | Thai term | National minimum (4-year) | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| General education | หมวดวิชาศึกษาทั่วไป | not fewer than 30 credits under the 2015 criteria; the 2022 criteria permit not fewer than 24 | Languages, humanities, social sciences, science and mathematics, life skills |
| Specific / specialist courses | หมวดวิชาเฉพาะ | not fewer than 72 credits | Subdivided into core (foundational), major required, and major elective; may include a minor |
| Free electives | หมวดวิชาเลือกเสรี | not fewer than 6 credits | Any module the student may register for, subject to prerequisites |

An **illustrative** 132-credit four-year Bachelor of Science programme might therefore be composed of: general education 30 credits; specific courses 96 credits (core 30, major required 51, major elective 15); free electives 6 credits. A student who accumulates 132 credits but takes only 4 credits of free electives has *not* fulfilled the requirements, even though the total is met — the composition is checked group by group, not merely in aggregate. This is one of the most frequent causes of a graduation application being returned.

## 2. The Grading System

### 2.1 Letter Grades and Grade Points

| Grade | Grade points | Conventional meaning | Counts in GPA? |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 4.0 | Excellent | Yes |
| B+ | 3.5 | Very good | Yes |
| B | 3.0 | Good | Yes |
| C+ | 2.5 | Fairly good | Yes |
| C | 2.0 | Fair | Yes |
| D+ | 1.5 | Poor | Yes |
| D | 1.0 | Very poor (lowest pass) | Yes |
| F | 0.0 | Fail | Yes — the credits count in the denominator |

The critical asymmetry is at the bottom of the scale. A **D** is a pass: the credits are earned and count towards the total. An **F** earns no credits towards the degree, but its credit value still enters the denominator of the grade point average, which is why a single failed four-credit module damages the average far more than most students expect.

### 2.2 Non-Numeric Marks

| Mark | Meaning | Effect on credits | Effect on GPA / GPAX |
|---|---|---|---|
| S | Satisfactory | Credits earned | Excluded from the calculation entirely |
| U | Unsatisfactory | No credits earned | Excluded from the calculation |
| I | Incomplete | Provisional; no credits until resolved | Excluded whilst outstanding; converts to a letter grade, or automatically to F if not resolved within the prescribed period (commonly the following semester) |
| W | Withdrawn | No credits | Excluded; the module appears on the transcript but does not affect the average |
| P | In progress (*กำลังศึกษา*) | Provisional; used for multi-semester projects, theses and co-operative education | Excluded until a final grade replaces it |
| Au | Audit | No credits | Excluded |

S and U are used for modules assessed on a pass basis — physical education, some seminar modules, certain activity or practicum modules, and non-credit remedial language modules. Because S/U modules are excluded from the average, a student cannot raise a weak GPAX by taking them, a point students regularly misunderstand.

## 3. Calculating the Grade Point Average

Two figures are maintained. The **semester grade point average (GPA)** covers one semester only. The **cumulative grade point average (GPAX)** covers every graded module the student has ever registered for. Both use the same formula:

> GPA = (Σ credits × grade points) ÷ (Σ credits of graded modules)

Only modules carrying A through F enter either sum. Results are conventionally truncated or rounded to two decimal places according to institutional practice; this guide rounds to two places.

### 3.1 Worked Example — Semester 1 (illustrative student, Miss Kanokwan P.)

| Module | Credits | Grade | Grade points | Credits × points |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| General Education: Thai Society and Culture | 3 | A | 4.0 | 12.0 |
| English for Communication I | 3 | B+ | 3.5 | 10.5 |
| Calculus I | 3 | C | 2.0 | 6.0 |
| General Physics | 4 | C+ | 2.5 | 10.0 |
| Introduction to Computing | 3 | B | 3.0 | 9.0 |
| Physical Education | 1 | S | — | excluded |

Graded credits = 3 + 3 + 3 + 4 + 3 = **16**. Total credit points = 12.0 + 10.5 + 6.0 + 10.0 + 9.0 = **47.5**. Semester 1 GPA = 47.5 ÷ 16 = 2.96875 ≈ **2.97**. She has earned 17 credits (the S-graded physical education module counts towards the degree total even though it is excluded from the average).

### 3.2 Worked Example — Semester 2, including a failure

| Module | Credits | Grade | Grade points | Credits × points |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Calculus II | 3 | B | 3.0 | 9.0 |
| Data Structures | 3 | A | 4.0 | 12.0 |
| Digital Logic Design | 3 | B+ | 3.5 | 10.5 |
| Probability and Statistics | 3 | F | 0.0 | 0.0 |
| English for Communication II | 3 | C+ | 2.5 | 7.5 |

Graded credits = **15**; credit points = **39.0**; Semester 2 GPA = 39.0 ÷ 15 = **2.60**. Note the arithmetic damage: had she achieved a C in Statistics, the semester GPA would have been 45.0 ÷ 15 = 3.00.

Cumulative position after two semesters: total graded credits = 16 + 15 = **31**; total credit points = 47.5 + 39.0 = **86.5**; **GPAX = 86.5 ÷ 31 = 2.7903 ≈ 2.79**. Credits earned towards the degree = 17 + 12 = 29 (the failed three credits are not earned).

### 3.3 Worked Example — Semester 3, including a repeated module

She re-registers for Probability and Statistics, which is compulsory in her programme and therefore must be repeated until passed.

| Module | Credits | Grade | Grade points | Credits × points |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Probability and Statistics (repeat) | 3 | C+ | 2.5 | 7.5 |
| Database Systems | 3 | A | 4.0 | 12.0 |
| Computer Networks | 3 | B | 3.0 | 9.0 |
| Software Engineering | 3 | B+ | 3.5 | 10.5 |
| Operating Systems | 3 | C+ | 2.5 | 7.5 |

Graded credits = **15**; credit points = **46.5**; Semester 3 GPA = 46.5 ÷ 15 = **3.10**.

The cumulative figure now depends on which repeat convention the institution applies, and this is where students most often miscalculate.

- **Convention A — all attempts counted (the usual Thai public-university practice).** Every registration with a letter grade remains in the calculation, and the F remains visible on the transcript. Total graded credits = 31 + 15 = **46**; total credit points = 86.5 + 46.5 = **133.0**; **GPAX = 133.0 ÷ 46 = 2.8913 ≈ 2.89**.
- **Convention B — latest attempt only.** Some institutions, or some programmes for designated modules, replace the earlier attempt. Removing the failed attempt gives graded credits = 46 − 3 = **43** and credit points unchanged at 133.0; **GPAX = 133.0 ÷ 43 = 3.0930 ≈ 3.09**.

The difference of 0.20 grade points from a single three-credit failure is not trivial, and under Convention A the failure is permanent: no amount of later success removes it from the denominator. Students should establish which convention applies to them before assuming a recovery plan will work.

## 4. Academic Standing and Minimum GPAX

The universal graduation threshold across Thai higher education is a **cumulative GPAX of not less than 2.00**. Programmes may impose additional module-level minima — for example, a minimum grade of C in specified professional modules in Nursing, Pharmacy or Education programmes, or a minimum GPAX in the major group considered separately.

Between enrolment and graduation, standing is monitored each regular semester. The pattern below is typical of Thai public universities and is given here as an **illustrative** statement of the structure rather than a quotation of UBU's current thresholds.

| Standing | Typical trigger | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Normal | GPAX ≥ 2.00 | None |
| Probation (*รอพินิจ* / วิทยาทัณฑ์) | GPAX below 2.00 at the end of a regular semester, from the second regular semester onwards | Registration capped (commonly at 15 credits); compulsory advising; the student must raise the GPAX |
| Dismissal / loss of student status (*พ้นสภาพ*) | GPAX below 1.50 at the end of the second regular semester or any later regular semester; **or** GPAX below 1.75 for two consecutive regular semesters after the second year; **or** exceeding the maximum study duration | Removal from the programme |

**Worked example — a student who falls below the threshold.** Mr Somchai T. completes Semester 1 with 18 graded credits and 28.8 credit points, giving a GPA and GPAX of 28.8 ÷ 18 = **1.60**. He is placed on probation. In Semester 2 he registers for 18 credits and earns 23.4 credit points, a semester GPA of 23.4 ÷ 18 = **1.30**. His cumulative position is (28.8 + 23.4) ÷ (18 + 18) = 52.2 ÷ 36 = **1.45**. Because this falls below 1.50 at the end of the second regular semester, he faces dismissal under the typical rule, notwithstanding that his first semester alone would only have warranted probation.

**Recovery arithmetic.** Had Mr Somchai instead reached a cumulative 1.72 after two semesters (61.92 points over 36 credits), he would remain on probation. To reach GPAX 2.00 across a third semester of 18 credits he would need total points of 2.00 × 54 = 108.0, that is 108.0 − 61.92 = **46.08 points from 18 credits**, a semester GPA of 2.56. This kind of calculation — "what do I need next semester?" — is worth teaching explicitly, because students consistently underestimate how quickly a poor start becomes arithmetically difficult to reverse: the denominator grows with every semester, so each new semester moves the cumulative figure less than the last.

## 5. Study Duration

Two limits apply simultaneously.

**Maximum duration.** A student may not remain enrolled for longer than **twice the normal programme length**, counted in academic years from first enrolment. A four-year programme therefore allows a maximum of eight academic years; a five-year programme ten; a six-year programme twelve. Summer sessions and semesters spent on approved leave of absence are normally counted within this window, which is why repeated leave is dangerous.

*Illustrative worked example.* A student first enrols in Semester 1 of academic year 2022 (June 2022) on a four-year, 132-credit programme. Twice the normal length is eight academic years: 2022, 2023, 2024, 2025, 2026, 2027, 2028 and 2029. Academic year 2029 ends around May 2030. The student must therefore have fulfilled every graduation requirement by the end of the second semester of academic year 2029 — approximately **May 2030**. A student who is still three credits short at that point loses student status regardless of an otherwise excellent GPAX.

**Minimum duration.** A student may not graduate in fewer than **six regular semesters** on a four-year programme (eight on a five-year, ten on a six-year), excluding summer sessions. Accelerating through heavy loads and summer registration can therefore compress a four-year degree to three years, but no further. This minimum exists partly to protect the integrity of prerequisite chains and partly because professional accreditation bodies require a minimum period of study.

## 6. Requirements Beyond Credits and GPAX

Fulfilling the credit composition and the 2.00 GPAX is necessary but not sufficient. Thai universities routinely attach further conditions, and UBU's programmes follow this pattern. The specific instruments and thresholds below are **illustrative of the general practice**; the current requirement for any given cohort is set out in that cohort's programme specification.

- **English proficiency.** Since MHESI's policy directive requiring every university to adopt an English language policy for undergraduates, Thai universities operate an exit assessment, commonly benchmarked to **CEFR B1** for bachelor's graduates. This may be satisfied by an institutional exit examination, or by an accepted external score (TOEIC, IELTS, TOEFL, CU-TEP, TU-GET and similar). Students who do not reach the benchmark are normally required to complete a remedial course and re-sit rather than being refused graduation outright.
- **Digital / IT proficiency.** Many institutions require an information-technology competency assessment, either an in-house test or an external certification.
- **Student activity hours.** Faculties commonly require a minimum number of recorded co-curricular activity hours across the programme, distributed amongst categories such as citizenship and morality, leadership and management, health and recreation, and academic development. A commonly cited **illustrative** figure is not fewer than 100 hours over the whole programme.
- **Co-operative education or professional practice.** Where the programme includes *sahakit sueksa* (co-operative education), a professional internship, clinical placement, teaching practicum or a professional-registration practicum, that component must be completed and graded before graduation. These components are frequently recorded with the P mark until the placement report is assessed, which means a student can appear "finished" in the credit count whilst still carrying an unresolved P.
- **Clearance of obligations.** The student must have no outstanding tuition or fee debt, must have returned all library materials and laboratory equipment, and must have settled any loan-fund or hall-of-residence obligations. Graduation approval is normally withheld until the relevant offices confirm clearance.

## 7. Honours (*เกียรตินิยม*)

### 7.1 Thresholds and Disqualifying Conditions

The standard Thai pattern awards two classes of honours on the basis of the final GPAX:

| Award | Typical GPAX threshold |
|---|---|
| First-class honours (*เกียรตินิยมอันดับหนึ่ง*) | not less than **3.50** |
| Second-class honours (*เกียรตินิยมอันดับสอง*) | not less than **3.25** |

Meeting the threshold alone is not enough. The standard disqualifying conditions, applied cumulatively, are:

1. **Duration.** The student must complete the programme within the **normal** length (four years for a four-year programme), not merely within the maximum permitted length.
2. **Failure.** The student must never have received an **F**. Many programmes extend this to "never received a grade below C" or to a U in any S/U module.
3. **Repetition.** The student must never have repeated a module — including repeating a passed module in order to improve the grade, where the regulations permit that at all.
4. **Transferred credits.** Students admitted with transferred credits from another institution, or by transfer from another programme, are generally ineligible. A related rule commonly requires that not fewer than three-quarters of the total credits be earned at the awarding university.

### 7.2 Worked Examples

**Student C — qualifies.** Miss Napatsorn completes a 132-credit four-year programme in eight regular semesters plus one summer session (still within academic year four, so within the normal duration). Final GPAX **3.58**. Lowest grade ever received: C. No repeats, no transferred credits, no F. She is awarded **first-class honours**.

**Student D — narrowly does not qualify, despite a higher average.** Mr Thanakrit finishes the same programme in eight regular semesters with a final GPAX of **3.61** — higher than Miss Napatsorn's. However, in his second semester he received an F in Probability and Statistics and repeated it in the summer session, achieving an A. Two disqualifying conditions are triggered: he has received an F, and he has repeated a module. He graduates **without honours**. The comparison makes the principle vivid: honours in the Thai system recognise an unbroken record, not merely a high arithmetic mean.

**Student E — second class.** Miss Chanidapa finishes within four years with GPAX **3.30**, no F, no repeats, no transferred credits. She receives **second-class honours**. Had her GPAX reached 3.50 she would have received first class; at 3.24 she would have received none, which is why final-semester module choices matter disproportionately to students sitting just under a boundary.

**Student F — disqualified by duration.** Mr Adisak achieves GPAX **3.52** with no F and no repeats, but took a semester of approved leave for medical reasons and completed in four and a half years. Because he exceeded the normal programme length, he graduates **without honours**, even though he satisfies every other condition. Some universities allow a documented medical or force-majeure exception here; students in that position should apply in writing rather than assume.

## 8. The Administrative Process

Graduation is an administrative act, not an automatic consequence of passing modules. The sequence is:

1. **Application for graduation.** The student submits a graduation request (*คำร้องขอสำเร็จการศึกษา*) to the Office of the Registrar, normally within the first few weeks of the semester in which the final modules will be completed. Students who forget to apply are commonly deferred to the following semester's approval cycle even though their academic work is finished — a costly clerical error, particularly for those with employment or postgraduate offers contingent on a certificate.
2. **Registrar's audit.** The Registrar verifies the credit composition group by group, the GPAX, the duration, the proficiency assessments, the activity hours, and the clearance of debts and library materials.
3. **Faculty board scrutiny.** The faculty's academic committee, and then the faculty board, review the audited list and endorse those who have fulfilled the requirements, together with any honours recommendations.
4. **University Council approval.** The final act of conferral is the approval of the **University Council** (*สภามหาวิทยาลัย*), which meets on a fixed cycle (commonly monthly). Only the Council can confer a degree.
5. **Issue of documents.** After Council approval, the Registrar issues the **transcript** and the **certificate of graduation**; the formal **degree certificate** (*ปริญญาบัตร*) is conferred at the graduation ceremony, which in Thailand is frequently held some months after approval.

### 8.1 Two Different Dates

Transcripts record two distinct dates and confusing them causes real problems.

- The **date of completing the requirements** (*วันที่สำเร็จการศึกษา*) is the date on which the final academic requirement was met — in practice, the last day of the examination period, or the date the final outstanding grade was recorded.
- The **date of Council approval** (*วันที่สภามหาวิทยาลัยอนุมัติ*) is the later date on which the degree was formally conferred.

A student who finishes in mid-March may not be approved until the Council meeting in, say, **15 May 2026**. Employers and postgraduate admissions offices differ on which date they require: civil-service and professional-registration applications typically require the approval date, whilst some employers accept a letter certifying completion. Whilst awaiting approval a student may request a certifying letter from the Registrar confirming that all requirements have been fulfilled and that the case is pending Council approval.

## 9. Summary: Graduation Checklist

| # | Requirement | How it is verified |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Total credits met (e.g. not fewer than 120; 132 in the illustrative programme) | Registrar's credit audit |
| 2 | Each credit group satisfied separately: general education, specific courses, free electives | Group-by-group audit against the programme specification |
| 3 | All compulsory modules passed, with any programme-specific minimum grades | Transcript check |
| 4 | Cumulative GPAX not less than 2.00 | Registrar's calculation |
| 5 | Duration within twice the normal programme length, and not fewer than the minimum semesters | Enrolment record |
| 6 | Student status normal (not dismissed, not on unresolved probation at the point of completion) | Standing record |
| 7 | English proficiency requirement satisfied | Language centre record or accepted external score |
| 8 | IT / digital proficiency requirement satisfied, where the programme imposes one | Assessment record |
| 9 | Required student-activity hours completed | Student affairs record |
| 10 | Co-operative education, internship, practicum or clinical placement completed and graded (no outstanding P) | Faculty record |
| 11 | No unresolved I marks | Transcript check |
| 12 | All debts cleared; library materials and equipment returned | Finance and library clearance |
| 13 | Graduation application submitted on time | Registrar's record |
| 14 | Faculty board endorsement and University Council approval obtained | Council minutes |

## 10. Common Misunderstandings Amongst Students

**"I passed every module, so I will graduate with honours."** Passing is a much weaker condition than qualifying for honours. Honours require a GPAX of 3.50 or 3.25 *and* an unbroken record: no F, no repeats, no transferred credits, and completion within the normal duration. Student D in Section 7.2 passed everything in the end, achieved 3.61, and still graduated without honours.

**"A withdrawal (W) hurts my GPAX."** It does not. A W is excluded from both numerator and denominator. What it does affect is the credit total and hence the time to graduation, and — in programmes where the module is compulsory and offered only once a year — the whole subsequent sequence. Withdrawing to avoid a likely D+ is arithmetically sensible; withdrawing repeatedly is a duration problem in slow motion.

**"Retaking the module removes the F."** Under the usual Thai practice (Convention A in Section 3.3) it does not. The F remains on the transcript and its credits remain in the GPAX denominator forever. Retaking earns the credits and adds new grade points, which raises the average, but it does not erase the original entry — and it independently disqualifies the student from honours.

**"S grades will pull my average up."** They cannot. S and U are excluded from the calculation entirely. Registering for extra S/U modules adds credits and workload without moving the GPAX by a single point.

**"An I will sort itself out."** An Incomplete converts automatically to an **F** if it is not resolved within the prescribed period. Students who leave an I outstanding whilst on placement or during a long holiday are frequently surprised. Equally, an unresolved I or P blocks the graduation audit even when the student's credit total looks complete.

**"I finished in March, so my degree is dated March."** The completion date and the Council approval date are different, and the document an employer requires may reference either. Check before making commitments that depend on the date.

**"My total credits are enough, so the composition does not matter."** It does. A student with 132 credits but only 4 free-elective credits, or 28 rather than 30 general-education credits, has not fulfilled the requirements and will have the graduation application returned. Audit the composition against the programme specification at the end of every year, not in the final semester.

**"Leave of absence stops the clock."** Ordinarily it does not. Semesters spent on approved leave normally count within the maximum study duration, so repeated leave consumes the eight-year window just as enrolled semesters do.
